A unified framework for functional, adversarial, cyber, and hardware verification of learned robot systems

Scope. Manipulation and station-based mobile manipulation. Locomotion, whole-body balance and public-road autonomy are out of scope.

Abstract

Learned manipulation policies are now updated on the cadence of software, shipped to fleets monthly, and evaluated with the rigour of a demo. Classical machine-safety verification assumes a deterministic controller whose behaviour can be argued from its structure; a vision-language-action policy offers neither determinism nor structure, and its competence is a property of a distribution rather than of a program. The decision to deploy a new policy version is consequently made today without a coherent body of evidence.

The evidence bearing on that decision already exists in four separate literatures and four separate industrial practices — functional evaluation, adversarial robustness, product cybersecurity, and hardware reliability — and no framework composes them. We propose one: a test taxonomy for the release decision, a fidelity-gating principle, a release-scoped immutable evidence model mapped to ISO 10218:2025 and EU Cyber Resilience Act obligations, and an evaluation protocol for the framework itself.

1The rule

Fidelity gating

A test may block a release only once its predictive validity against real outcomes has been measured. Otherwise it reports as advisory. Apparent realism is not a qualification.

This matters more in robotics than in software testing, because a robotics test can look extremely convincing and still be uninformative. A photorealistic scene with guessed friction coefficients produces confident, wrong rankings — which is precisely the configuration current automated real-to-sim pipelines ship in, since they obtain mass and friction by querying a vision-language model rather than measuring them.

Fidelity gating also makes low-fidelity tests safe to keep. A cable-routing suite at ρ = 0.5 still surfaces a large regression worth a real-world check; under a naive gate it would either block releases spuriously or be deleted.

4How to test the framework

  1. Retrospective gate study. Take a fleet's last N checkpoints, their real outcomes and known field regressions. Build suites blind to the outcomes, compute ρ, apply the gate, and report rank correlation, catch rate and false-block rate. This is the load-bearing experiment.
  2. Adversarial pre/post. Instantiate four published attack categories, measure success, apply a guard, re-measure, and report simulated-to-physical transfer.
  3. Cross-family interaction. Measure functional and attack success jointly; stratify functional failures by hardware degradation index.
  4. Budget allocation. With fixed physical-cell hours, compare uniform, fidelity-weighted and uncertainty-weighted allocation against random sampling.

5The dependency that decides it

The functional leg rests on success detection, and published success detectors are near chance exactly where industrial value concentrates. We believe the missing evidence is non-visual — wrist force/torque, joint effort, vacuum pressure, tactile — and that verifier design should be multimodal rather than vision-only. That is a claim in this paper, not a result.

The remaining open problems, treated in §7: calibrating fidelity without historical checkpoints, identifying physical parameters from interaction logs instead of language-model priors, the compute budget of verification-time scaling, standards coverage for learned components, and data protection as a constraint on sharing evidence.
